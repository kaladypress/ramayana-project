1: After hearing Nārada's words, the great sage Vālmīki — a master of speech and a soul devoted to dharma — honored him, together with his disciples.
2: Honored in the proper way, the divine sage Nārada asked leave of Vālmīki, and once he was given permission, he rose into the sky.
3: A short while after Nārada had left for the world of the devatās, the sage Vālmīki went to the bank of the Tamasā, not far from the Jāhnavī (Gaṅgā).
4: Reaching the bank of the Tamasā, the great sage saw a bathing place free of mud, and he spoke to the disciple standing beside him:
5: "Look, Bharadvāja — this bathing place has no mud at all. It's lovely, and its water is as clear as the mind of a good person.
6: Set down the water pot, my son, and hand me my bark garment. I will bathe right here, in this fine spot on the Tamasā."
7: When the great-souled Vālmīki said this, the disciplined Bharadvāja handed his teacher, the sage, his bark garment.
8: Taking the bark garment from his disciple's hand, the sage, master of his senses, walked about, gazing at the vast forest all around him.
9: Close by, the revered sage saw a pair of Krauñca birds moving about together, never apart from each other, calling sweetly.
10: Then, as the sage watched, a Niṣāda hunter — full of hostility and set on doing evil — struck down the male of that pair.
11: Seeing her mate killed, rolling on the ground, his body covered in blood, the female cried out in a pitiful voice.
12: Separated from that mate of hers — her constant winged companion, copper-crested and intoxicated with love, who had been united with her — she cried out in sorrow.
13: When the sage, whose soul was rooted in dharma, saw the bird struck down by the Niṣāda in this way, he was overcome with compassion.
14: Moved by that compassion, and seeing the female Krauñca weeping, the brāhmaṇa thought, "This is wrong," and spoke these words:
15: "Niṣāda, may you never find a place of rest for endless years, because you killed one of this pair of Krauñcas while he was lost in love."
16: Even as he spoke these words and looked on, a thought arose in his heart: "Overcome with grief for this bird, what is this I have said?"
17: Reflecting on it, the wise and thoughtful sage reached a clear understanding. Then that foremost of sages said this to his disciple:
18: "Bound in metrical feet, with an equal number of syllables in each, and fit to be sung to the rhythm of the strings — let what came from me in my grief (śōka) be called a śloka, and nothing else."
19: The disciple joyfully took in those excellent words as the sage spoke them, and his teacher was pleased with him.
20: Then the sage bathed at that spot in the prescribed manner, and turned back, still thinking over that same matter.
21: Then his disciple Bharadvāja — humble and learned — took up the full water pot and followed behind his teacher.
22: Entering the āśrama with his disciple, the sage who knew dharma sat down, spoke of other matters, and then settled into meditation.
23: Then Brahmā himself came there — the four-faced, radiant creator of the worlds, the Supreme One — to see that foremost of sages.
24: When Vālmīki saw him, he rose at once, speechless and utterly amazed. He stood with folded hands, composed and reverent.
25: He honored that god with water for his feet, the respectful offering (arghya), a seat, and words of praise. He bowed to him in the proper way and asked after the well-being of the eternal one.
26: The revered Brahmā then sat on a seat of the highest honor and gestured for the great sage Vālmīki to take a seat as well.
27: Even as Brahmā, grandsire of the worlds, sat there before him in person, Vālmīki sank into thought, his mind fixed on what had happened:
28: "What a terrible thing that evil man did, his mind set on hostility — to kill such a sweetly singing Krauñca for no reason at all!"
29: Grieving again and again for the female Krauñca, lost in sorrow and turned inward, he sang that verse once more.
30: Then Brahmā smiled and said to that foremost of sages, "What you have composed is truly a śloka. There's no need to doubt it.
31: Brāhmaṇa, this flow of speech, this Sarasvatī, came to you by my will alone. Best of r̥ṣis, now compose the whole story of Rāma.
32: Tell the story of Rāma — wise, steadfast, virtuous, and devoted to dharma — just as you heard it from Nārada.
33: Everything that happened to the wise Rāma and to Saumitri (Lakṣmaṇa), whether hidden or in the open, and all that concerns the Rākṣasas,
34: and everything that happened to Vaidēhī (Sītā), whether in public or in private — even what is still unknown to you will all become known to you.
35: Nothing you say in this poem will be untrue. Compose the sacred and beautiful story of Rāma in ślokas.
36: As long as the mountains stand and the rivers flow on the earth, the story of the Rāmāyaṇa will spread through the worlds.
37: And as long as the story of Rāma that you compose continues to spread, you will live in my worlds, above and below."
38: After saying this, the revered Brahmā vanished right there. Then the sage Vālmīki and his disciples were filled with wonder.
39: Then all his disciples sang that śloka again and again, delighting in it, and in their deep amazement they said:
40: "The grief the great sage expressed in four feet of equal syllables has, by being repeated, become a śloka."
41: Then a resolve formed in the mind of Vālmīki, whose soul was purified: "I will compose the entire Rāmāyaṇa poem in verses like these."
42: Then that renowned and noble-minded sage composed a poem that brings glory to the glorious Rāma — hundreds of ślokas with equal syllables, with beautiful words and lofty meaning and style.
43: Listen with devotion to this epic composed by the sage — richly adorned with elegant compounds and euphony, sweet and profound in meaning — recounting the noble life of the foremost of the Raghus and the destruction of the ten-headed Rāvaṇa.
